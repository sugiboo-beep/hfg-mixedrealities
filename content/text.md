## The Floor is Lava_1  (the-floor-is-lava_1)

The Floor is Lava

Which strategies do we develop when the ground beneath us is no longer stable? Which narratives shape our understanding of crises and the possibility of renewal?
This seminar approaches apocalyptic imaginaries through artistic, theoretical, and experimental lenses. We examine who holds the power to define apocalypses and if our notions of community, technology and infrastructure shift in times of collapse. Navigating the intersections of political, ecological, and technological upheaval, we are exploring collective practices that arise when existing systems falter.
The seminar takes place in collaboration with UMBAU and includes a field trip to an active volcano. Students are invited to create individual or collaborative works and experiment with the format of the online journal UMBAU, where these works will be published.

## Frameworks of Fiction  (frameworks-of-fiction)

Frameworks of Fiction

How do cultural representations contribute to the production of reality and historiography? Which voices and stories are missing within Eurocentric narratives and exhibition productions? And how does fragmented knowledge of the past distort our understanding of the present—and the futures we imagine? For this seminar we will focus on historical paintings, working with the collection of the Kunsthalle Mannheim and examine collection interests, omissions and story telling forms. Our point of departure is Édouard Manet’s seminal history painting L'exécution de l'empereur Maximilien, which captures a moment of colonial violence between the French Empire and Mexican Republicans. From here, we will explore artistic responses that disrupt dominant readings—through performances, immersive media, interactive games, moving images, or XR interventions. These interventions will be shown in Kunsthalle Mannheim on June 18th and are embedded within the framework ‘The Missing Stories. Re-Mapping History in Contemporary Art’, in conjunction with the exhibition by the artist Tavares Strachan opening in April 2025.

## Hybrid Biennale Hellerau - Theft as a political strategy  (hybrid-biennale-hellerau-theft-as-a-political-strategy)

Hybrid Biennale Hellerau - Theft as a political strategy

In Kooperation mit dem Artificial Museum und HELLERAU haben sich in einem von Charlotte Eifler geleiteten Seminar 'Diebstahl als politische Strategie' Studierende der HfG Karlsruhe im Sommersemster 2022 kritisch mit dem konventionellen Begriff des Diebstahl anhand digitaler Artefakte beschäftigt.

Die künstlerischen Arbeiten von David Marcelino Cayetano & Alejandra Janus, Jule Köpke, Wiebke Müller, Luise Peschko, Janis Zeckai und Charlotte Eifler befassen sich mit Themen der Raubkunst, Inflation, des Zeitdiebstahls sowie den Shoplifting communities.

## Disruptive Worlding  (disruptive-worlding)

Disruptive Worlding

„It matters what matters we use to think other matters with; it matters what stories we tell to tell other stories with; it matters what knots knot knots, what thoughts think thoughts, what descriptions describe descriptions, what ties tie ties." Donna Haraway Worldbuilding ist eine Methode, die zur Gestaltung von Science-Fiction- und Fantasy-Erzählungen in Film, Videospielen und Literatur verwendet wird. Es handelt sich um eine Praxis, bei der eine holistische Welt entworfen wird, aus der eine unendliche Anzahl von Erzählungen entspringen können. Ausgehend von Gayatri Spivaks Konzept des Worldings werden wir digitale und analoge Artefakte generieren, die als Startpunkte für eigene alternative Zukünfte fungieren. Durch die Entwicklung konkreter Artefakte werden die Details unserer geschaffenen disruptiven Welten zum Leben erweckt. Fiktion, Storytelling und Szenografie stehen im Fokus dieses Seminars. Die Teilnehmenden sind eingeladen, eigene Projekte unterschiedlicher Formate zu realisieren. Disruptive Worlding II ist eine Fortsetzung des gleichnamigen Seminars aus dem letzten Semester von Charlotte Eifler und wird dieses Semester in Zusammenarbeit mit Ebba Fransén Walhör und der Kuratorin Agnieszka Roguski durchgeführt. Es beinhaltet eine Exkursion zu der Gruppenausstellung WORLDBUILDING: Videospiele und Kunst im digitalen Zeitalter in Düsseldorf Anfang Mai.

## Grand Hotel Waldlust  (grand-hotel-waldlust)

Grand Hotel Waldlust

The seminar is the continuation of a critical and playful exploration on the legacy and contemporary status of grand hotels. Which ghosts are haunting us in the spaces of classicist and colonial luxury, the chambers of care work and hospitality and meeting places of artists and wealthy bourgeoisie? Which futures could emerge out of a pool combining various temporalities? In collaboration with the Waldlust e.V., a former Grand Hotel in Freudenstadt, we are developing projects in the field of new media, film, photography and installation. The works will be shown in an exhibition from July-September 2024, along contributions from ABK Stuttgart and Hochschule Offenburg

## Remember the Future - NSU Komplex auflösen  (remember-the-future-nsu-komplex-auflösen)

Remember the Future - NSU Komplex auflösen

In collaboration with Staatsarchiv Chemnitz, ASA FF and the project NSU - Offener Prozess, the students are invited to to research and work on the conception of a digital memorial site in commemoration of the victims and those affected by the NSU complex. The project is both located online and on site, highlighting the political entanglements supporting these atrocities within the city of Chemnitz. Re:member the future is possibly accompanied by an exhibition in Chemnitz, Kulturhauptstadt 2025. From 2001 to 2010, the neo-Nazis of the NSU carried out a series of racially motivated murders and attacks with a broad support network from the far-right scene in Jena, Chemnitz and Zwickau. The project and exhibition "Offener Prozess” is concerned with the NSU complex. It takes the East German reality, particularly in Saxony, as a starting point to tell a story of the NSU complex that is based on the stories of migration and the continuities of right-wing and racist violence and resistance to it. With the approach of "living memory", it highlights marginalized perspectives and focuses on structural and institutional racism.

## Daeyong Lee  (daeyong-lee)

Practicing Uncertainty

Daeyong Lee

The journey to Stromboli did not go as planned. Ferries were cancelled, schedules were broken and the exploration to Stromboli became uncertain. Being stranded somewhere without a place to sleep was frightening. However something meaningful quietly grew from that chaos. As we improvised and found new solutions, a collective instinct seemed to take shape. Decisions were made at presence, not through certainty. We were looking at one another, seeking possibilities and acting without overthinking. I learned that things don’t always happen as planned, and sometimes it’s more meaningful when the illusion of control breaks down.

Due to the changing ferry schedule and unpredictable weather, we spontaneously agreed to explore a nearby volcanic island, Vulcano. As the island thrives during the summer season, most of the businesses were temporarily shut down. There was a sense of emptiness with a small local community keeping company on the island. Just upon arrival to the top of the crater, a storm arrived suddenly, almost theatrically. Dark clouds covered over the island, the wind bursted in violence, and rain began to strike. It was not the kind of storm that kills, but it was the kind that reminds you of your uncontrollable limits. Standing against the storm, I felt something vast and terrifying of the scale of nature, a sense of non-linguistic force and a recognition of scale that has no interest in human intentions. Nature does not warn, threaten, or negotiate. It simply exists.

That feeling stayed with me as we moved toward Stromboli the next day. As we approached the viewing point of the breathing volcano, I noticed the wooden deck beneath our feet. An engineered, leveled, construction I would never know. A human made structure that allowed me to stand safely in front of a breathing natural phenomenon we could never control. Even so, in the darkness, with the sea behind us and the mountain rising, I felt as though I had entered untouched nature.

Stromboli felt raw and unmediated. Yet the conditions that allowed me to experience it were the carefully managed trails, safety regulations, invisible infrastructure. It made me realize how often our sense of ‘contact with nature’ is made possible by layers of human mediation. Even after acknowledging the engineered systems, the sense of awe I felt was never gone. Perhaps the illusion was not meant to deceive us. Instead, it made me think that we, as humans, might actually need these kinds of thresholds. We need these structures to support us just enough so that we can safely approach the core of nature and the power we otherwise could not face alone.

The eruption was quick and sudden. The buffered sound wasn’t the roar I expected. Rather, it was something hollow and sharp. It reminded me of sounds that come before language. A gasp before crying, the breath of someone angry, a burst of laughter and the deep inhale of pure emotion(also a sense of loneliness). I understood that nature exists as it is, however the fuming volcano seemed like an expression, whether or not I was projecting my own internal states onto it. I finally understood why the people of Stromboli consider the volcano as mother nature and perhaps why in the past the sense of spiritual emotions were stronger.

I guess this is why the experience left me thinking about human universals. Before seeing the volcano, I felt something that had little to do with nationality, culture, or ideology. It was the simple, shared knowledge of being small. Emotions like fear, awe, and curiosity were not cultural, they were just human. In a world where everyone is busy drawing lines to stay apart, it was strangely peaceful to be reminded of what we all share. Standing there, the weight of our common ground felt more real than any of our differences.

This feeling resonated with something lingering inside me for a long time before arriving in Germany and coming to Italy. I was born in South Korea, spent my childhood in Scotland and Germany, and returned to Korea as a teenager. Korea was relatively a homogenous society where similar standards are strongly embedded in the culture. For years, I wanted to explain my background just to justify who I was. Language played a powerful role in how identity was understood. Yet I struggled with every language I spoke. My Korean felt incomplete, unfamiliar with subtle social codes(often called “nunchi”), and my English & German remained at a childhood level. There was a period when I never felt confident in any language. I often found myself explaining myself instead of simply being understood. I thought about how I should adapt as an individual. At the same time, I was scared to make a voice and talk out loud. The two words in Korean, “being different(dareuda)” are often confused with “being wrong(teulida)”. The two words even sound similar in Korean, and that overlap shaped how I judged myself. I frequently asked: Am I living the right way? What if I am wrong just for being different?

As I grew older, my perspective on art and design became more complex. I began paying more attention to materiality, textures, craft, and the physical presence of objects. At times, I worried that this meant I was becoming shallow or overly focused on surface. But I slowly realized that valuing material quality is not the same as worshiping wealth. Still, the boundary often felt unclear, and I felt uneasy about the artistic world I was entering. I wondered whether it truly supports sincere exploration, or if it ultimately rewards those with access, status, or financial security.

Living within that tension, I often questioned my perception of others. Were the jealousy, arrogance, indifference, and quiet hostility I felt from people around me real? Or were they illusions created by my own insecurities? I could never fully answer the question. But on the volcano, the concern felt strangely distant. Human emotions such as envy, pride, and coldness seemed so small to the forces in front of me.

The volcano made me think about emotions that exist before language, something deeper than nationality, social roles, or cultural expectations. It softened some of the invisible boundaries I had built toward others, and toward myself. It reminded me that beneath language, identity, and politics, there is a shared core of human feeling: fear, awe, loneliness, curiosity, tenderness. These emotions felt universal, not cultural.

This raised questions about how society would change, how we would work, how we would survive and how art would evolve within such a climate. I felt both anticipation and fear. Thrilled because a shifting world might allow new forms of creativity to emerge but fear because fragile times shakes the stability for artists(everyone else too). For my generation and the one below it, the future feels less like a path and more like a fog. There is excitement in that uncertainty, but also a quiet terror. None of us knows how stable our grounds will be.

We live in a world of constant motion, acceleration, and noise.
Stories change before we fully settle into them. I do wonder if anything could be, or should be fully settled. Standing before something ancient reminded me that instability is not new. It has always been part of being human.

The volcano made me reconsider what kind of artist I want to become.
Someone aware of their limits. Someone humble. Someone who listens more than they assert. Someone who influences others through sincerity rather than authority.

## Sukyoung Lee  (sukyoung-lee)

Backbone on Unsteady Ground

Sukyoung Lee

1. A few months ago, I visited an orthopedic clinic to have an X-ray of my spine taken. They told me my spine curved slightly to the left. When scoliosis develops, it’s not just a matter of the vertebrae being uneven in height—there’s a three-dimensional distortion. One side of my body is always slightly higher than the other, and my torso is subtly rotated. Only with awareness can I face the exact forward. Balance is not something naturally given to my body. It is something I should adjust anew, each time.

> Are your head, your neck, your spine,
> and your lower body all facing the same direction right now?

2. After waiting out several days of rough seas, we finally reached Stromboli and climbed the volcano together. The volcanic layers, blown by the winds on Stromboli, are composed of extremely fine particles, so the ground shifts slightly with every step. The higher we climbed, the narrower the trail became, and the steeper the slope. The ground seems solid, yet it changes form under your feet. To maintain balance, you should lower your body, and shift your weight. You should accept that your weight is never fully divided between both feet.

> As you lower yourself to find balance, notice the tall grasses brushing your body.
> Since the soil their roots grip is fine and soft, the plants themselves are tender and delicate. Where do they touch your body?
> What traces do they leave?

3. During infant development, the spine plays a major role in establishing the body’s vertical axis. A unified vertical axis provides the energetic foundation for the experience of self—“I-am-ness”—and allows humans to establish a clear, stable relationship with gravity. Yet the spine is never meant to remain rigid. Its virtue lies in its flexibility. Anyone who has tried a handstand or headstand would know: keeping the spine centered does not mean staying still; it means constantly regaining balance after every sway. Breath, intra-abdominal pressure, the pelvic floor, and diaphragm negotiate continuously, making subtle adjustments that sustain this flexible equilibrium.

> Place the crown of your head on the ground.
> How does your head read the earth?
> Feel the sensation of your head and spine making contact with the ground,
> and observe how your entire body responds to this contact.

4. Some parts of the Earth hold colder air than others. That the air is cold reveals that the ground itself exists in relation to other grounds. From afar, this relationship may appear as currents in the wind, but the moment you stand upon it, it becomes a matter for the body. As the wind blows and waves rise, the seasickness on the boat to Stromboli, the slips along the volcanic trail – these are all ways the body experiences this relationship. Thus the volcanic ground is an unstable place to stand, a place that urges you to keep moving.

> On calm days, the sea and sky meet in a clear horizon
> How natural does this stark line feel to you?
> When you step onto the boat, the horizon wavers, and discrepancies arise
> between
> your ears, eyes, and muscles.
> How firm is your trust in balance, then?

5. Halfway along, I turned my head and saw the sea below my feet. Every step on the slippery soil felt heavy and precarious. Observing my stiffness, our guide from Naples said: “Start with your heels. Press the ground firmly and shift your weight slowly.” Trust gravity, he said. It eased the tension in both my body and mind, because it sounded like a reminder that instead of straightening my upper body and trying to take control, it was enough simply to lower my center. I didn’t have to struggle against the earth’s force—and that was a great relief.

> Where you are now, in this posture and position, trust gravity.

6. The instability of a volcano is not a property of a single place—it is a condition of the environment that our bodies must repeatedly navigate. If one is born with an unstable body (in whatever way that means), what does it mean to find balance on unsteady ground? How can we cultivate the practice not to resist sway, but to recalibrate after it? Is this a skill that ends with a single journey, or could it leave an imprint on the flesh, bones, and muscles of the bodies that come after mine?

> Imagine the ground beneath you trembling.
> Moving slowly across it.
> Remember how a swaying body finds its balance,
> and pass that wisdom on to those you love.

## Benedikt Endres  (benedikt-endres)

The Ground beneath

A game of Perspective and future

Dialog- und Regiefassung
Von Benedikt Endres

A group of players enters a game. So far so good. But this game is not what they expect. As they are strangers to each other and all originally planned to play something different, they suddenly land in an exit room, as part of that very random group they are in now.

Whatever higher entity controls that game or the exit room as whole, it is only clear that its intentions are rather malicious. To win and come back into reality, the group has to form a team and play games that are frighteningly real, solve tasks they never solved before, and most importantly: survive.

Thereby the group begins to question rules and authority, determines whether we can influence certain things in life, finds strategies for handling crises and finds a safe space and community.

“The Ground beneath” is a performance that is being created as part of the seminar “The Floor is Lava” at the HfG Karlsruhe and the associated excursion to the Stromboli volcano. There is also a connection to Issue 4 (title: “The Floor is Lava”) of the HfG's online journal “Umbau”, in which the first scene of the performance will be published.
