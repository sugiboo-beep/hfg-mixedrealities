The Floor is Lava

Backbone on Unsteady Ground

Sukyoung Lee

1. A few months ago, I visited an orthopedic clinic to have an X-ray of my spine taken. They told me my spine curved slightly to the left. When scoliosis develops, it’s not just a matter of the vertebrae being uneven in height—there’s a three-dimensional distortion. One side of my body is always slightly higher than the other, and my torso is subtly rotated. Only with awareness can I face the exact forward. Balance is not something naturally given to my body. It is something I should adjust anew, each time.

Are your head, your neck, your spine,
and your lower body all facing the same direction right now?

2. After waiting out several days of rough seas, we finally reached Stromboli and climbed the volcano together. The volcanic layers, blown by the winds on Stromboli, are composed of extremely fine particles, so the ground shifts slightly with every step. The higher we climbed, the narrower the trail became, and the steeper the slope. The ground seems solid, yet it changes form under your feet. To maintain balance, you should lower your body, and shift your weight. You should accept that your weight is never fully divided between both feet.

As you lower yourself to find balance, notice the tall grasses brushing your body.
Since the soil their roots grip is fine and soft, the plants themselves are tender and delicate. Where do they touch your body?
What traces do they leave?

3. During infant development, the spine plays a major role in establishing the body’s vertical axis. A unified vertical axis provides the energetic foundation for the experience of self—“I-am-ness”—and allows humans to establish a clear, stable relationship with gravity. Yet the spine is never meant to remain rigid. Its virtue lies in its flexibility. Anyone who has tried a handstand or headstand would know: keeping the spine centered does not mean staying still; it means constantly regaining balance after every sway. Breath, intra-abdominal pressure, the pelvic floor, and diaphragm negotiate continuously, making subtle adjustments that sustain this flexible equilibrium.

Place the crown of your head on the ground.
How does your head read the earth?
Feel the sensation of your head and spine making contact with the ground,
and observe how your entire body responds to this contact.

4. Some parts of the Earth hold colder air than others. That the air is cold reveals that the ground itself exists in relation to other grounds. From afar, this relationship may appear as currents in the wind, but the moment you stand upon it, it becomes a matter for the body. As the wind blows and waves rise, the seasickness on the boat to Stromboli, the slips along the volcanic trail – these are all ways the body experiences this relationship. Thus the volcanic ground is an unstable place to stand, a place that urges you to keep moving.

On calm days, the sea and sky meet in a clear horizon
How natural does this stark line feel to you?
When you step onto the boat, the horizon wavers, and discrepancies arise
between
your ears, eyes, and muscles.
How firm is your trust in balance, then?

5. Halfway along, I turned my head and saw the sea below my feet. Every step on the slippery soil felt heavy and precarious. Observing my stiffness, our guide from Naples said: “Start with your heels. Press the ground firmly and shift your weight slowly.” Trust gravity, he said. It eased the tension in both my body and mind, because it sounded like a reminder that instead of straightening my upper body and trying to take control, it was enough simply to lower my center. I didn’t have to struggle against the earth’s force—and that was a great relief.

Where you are now, in this posture and position, trust gravity.

6. The instability of a volcano is not a property of a single place—it is a condition of the environment that our bodies must repeatedly navigate. If one is born with an unstable body (in whatever way that means), what does it mean to find balance on unsteady ground? How can we cultivate the practice not to resist sway, but to recalibrate after it? Is this a skill that ends with a single journey, or could it leave an imprint on the flesh, bones, and muscles of the bodies that come after mine?

Imagine the ground beneath you trembling.
Moving slowly across it.
Remember how a swaying body finds its balance,
and pass that wisdom on to those you love.
