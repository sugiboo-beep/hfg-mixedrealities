The Floor is Lava

Practicing Uncertainty

Daeyong Lee

The journey to Stromboli did not go as planned. Ferries were cancelled, schedules were broken and the exploration to Stromboli became uncertain. Being stranded somewhere without a place to sleep was frightening. However something meaningful quietly grew from that chaos. As we improvised and found new solutions, a collective instinct seemed to take shape. Decisions were made at presence, not through certainty. We were looking at one another, seeking possibilities and acting without overthinking. I learned that things don’t always happen as planned, and sometimes it’s more meaningful when the illusion of control breaks down.

Due to the changing ferry schedule and unpredictable weather, we spontaneously agreed to explore a nearby volcanic island, Vulcano. As the island thrives during the summer season, most of the businesses were temporarily shut down. There was a sense of emptiness with a small local community keeping company on the island. Just upon arrival to the top of the crater, a storm arrived suddenly, almost theatrically. Dark clouds covered over the island, the wind bursted in violence, and rain began to strike. It was not the kind of storm that kills, but it was the kind that reminds you of your uncontrollable limits. Standing against the storm, I felt something vast and terrifying of the scale of nature, a sense of non-linguistic force and a recognition of scale that has no interest in human intentions. Nature does not warn, threaten, or negotiate. It simply exists.

That feeling stayed with me as we moved toward Stromboli the next day. As we approached the viewing point of the breathing volcano, I noticed the wooden deck beneath our feet. An engineered, leveled, construction I would never know. A human made structure that allowed me to stand safely in front of a breathing natural phenomenon we could never control. Even so, in the darkness, with the sea behind us and the mountain rising, I felt as though I had entered untouched nature.

Stromboli felt raw and unmediated. Yet the conditions that allowed me to experience it were the carefully managed trails, safety regulations, invisible infrastructure. It made me realize how often our sense of ‘contact with nature’ is made possible by layers of human mediation. Even after acknowledging the engineered systems, the sense of awe I felt was never gone. Perhaps the illusion was not meant to deceive us. Instead, it made me think that we, as humans, might actually need these kinds of thresholds. We need these structures to support us just enough so that we can safely approach the core of nature and the power we otherwise could not face alone.

The eruption was quick and sudden. The buffered sound wasn’t the roar I expected. Rather, it was something hollow and sharp. It reminded me of sounds that come before language. A gasp before crying, the breath of someone angry, a burst of laughter and the deep inhale of pure emotion(also a sense of loneliness). I understood that nature exists as it is, however the fuming volcano seemed like an expression, whether or not I was projecting my own internal states onto it. I finally understood why the people of Stromboli consider the volcano as mother nature and perhaps why in the past the sense of spiritual emotions were stronger.

I guess this is why the experience left me thinking about human universals. Before seeing the volcano, I felt something that had little to do with nationality, culture, or ideology. It was the simple, shared knowledge of being small. Emotions like fear, awe, and curiosity were not cultural, they were just human. In a world where everyone is busy drawing lines to stay apart, it was strangely peaceful to be reminded of what we all share. Standing there, the weight of our common ground felt more real than any of our differences.

This feeling resonated with something lingering inside me for a long time before arriving in Germany and coming to Italy. I was born in South Korea, spent my childhood in Scotland and Germany, and returned to Korea as a teenager. Korea was relatively a homogenous society where similar standards are strongly embedded in the culture. For years, I wanted to explain my background just to justify who I was. Language played a powerful role in how identity was understood. Yet I struggled with every language I spoke. My Korean felt incomplete, unfamiliar with subtle social codes(often called “nunchi”), and my English & German remained at a childhood level. There was a period when I never felt confident in any language. I often found myself explaining myself instead of simply being understood. I thought about how I should adapt as an individual. At the same time, I was scared to make a voice and talk out loud. The two words in Korean, “being different(dareuda)” are often confused with “being wrong(teulida)”. The two words even sound similar in Korean, and that overlap shaped how I judged myself. I frequently asked: Am I living the right way? What if I am wrong just for being different?

As I grew older, my perspective on art and design became more complex. I began paying more attention to materiality, textures, craft, and the physical presence of objects. At times, I worried that this meant I was becoming shallow or overly focused on surface. But I slowly realized that valuing material quality is not the same as worshiping wealth. Still, the boundary often felt unclear, and I felt uneasy about the artistic world I was entering. I wondered whether it truly supports sincere exploration, or if it ultimately rewards those with access, status, or financial security.

Living within that tension, I often questioned my perception of others. Were the jealousy, arrogance, indifference, and quiet hostility I felt from people around me real? Or were they illusions created by my own insecurities? I could never fully answer the question. But on the volcano, the concern felt strangely distant. Human emotions such as envy, pride, and coldness seemed so small to the forces in front of me.

The volcano made me think about emotions that exist before language, something deeper than nationality, social roles, or cultural expectations. It softened some of the invisible boundaries I had built toward others, and toward myself. It reminded me that beneath language, identity, and politics, there is a shared core of human feeling: fear, awe, loneliness, curiosity, tenderness. These emotions felt universal, not cultural.

This raised questions about how society would change, how we would work, how we would survive and how art would evolve within such a climate. I felt both anticipation and fear. Thrilled because a shifting world might allow new forms of creativity to emerge but fear because fragile times shakes the stability for artists(everyone else too). For my generation and the one below it, the future feels less like a path and more like a fog. There is excitement in that uncertainty, but also a quiet terror. None of us knows how stable our grounds will be.

We live in a world of constant motion, acceleration, and noise.
Stories change before we fully settle into them. I do wonder if anything could be, or should be fully settled. Standing before something ancient reminded me that instability is not new. It has always been part of being human.

The volcano made me reconsider what kind of artist I want to become.
Someone aware of their limits. Someone humble. Someone who listens more than they assert. Someone who influences others through sincerity rather than authority.
